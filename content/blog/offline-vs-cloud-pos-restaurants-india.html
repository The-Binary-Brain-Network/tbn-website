What the two words actually mean

Cloud POS keeps your data on the vendor's servers. The counter runs an app or a browser that talks to those servers over the internet. You usually pay monthly, per outlet or per terminal, and you can see your numbers from anywhere.

Offline-first POS keeps the database on a computer in your restaurant. The app reads and writes locally, so it never waits on a server. Internet is used for updates and optional extras. You usually pay a licence once a year.

Plenty of products sit in between: cloud systems with an offline mode that queues orders until the connection returns, and local systems that copy data to the cloud for backup or a remote dashboard. The useful question is not which label a product wears, but what it does in three situations.

Situation 1: the internet drops at 8.30 on a Saturday

This is the test that matters most, and it is easy to run during a demo: unplug the router and try to take an order, send it to the kitchen, bill the table and take UPI.

  • A well-built offline-first POS carries on exactly as before. KOTs print because the printer is on your local network or USB. Bills print. Cash and static UPI QR work. Only gateway-confirmed UPI waits for the connection.
  • A cloud POS with a proper offline mode will usually let you keep taking orders and printing, then sync later. Check whether bill numbers stay sequential when it syncs, and whether a second terminal can see the first terminal's orders while offline (often it cannot).
  • A browser-only cloud POS with no offline mode stops.

If your area has reliable fibre and a mobile hotspot as backup, this matters less. In much of India — hill stations, highway dhabas, older buildings in city centres — it decides the question on its own.

Situation 2: the owner wants numbers from home

This is where cloud wins clearly. A cloud POS shows live sales on a phone from anywhere, and a group with several outlets gets one head-office view without effort.

Offline-first systems have to do something extra to get numbers to the owner: a daily summary sent by email, a report printed at closing, or a separate cloud dashboard fed from the local database. These work, but they are not live. If watching sales minute by minute from another city matters to you, weigh that honestly.

Situation 3: five years later

A single outlet paying ₹1,500 a month for cloud POS spends ₹90,000 over five years, before add-ons. A self-hosted licence at ₹7,500 a year costs ₹37,500 over the same period. The gap widens with more outlets or terminals on per-unit pricing. Run your own numbers in the cost calculator.

The other five-year question is your data. With cloud POS, five years of sales history sits with the vendor; check you can export all of it, in a usable format, without asking. With offline POS it sits on your PC, which makes backups your concern — so check that the software makes them automatically.

Backups: the offline system's real obligation

If the database lives on your counter PC, a dead hard disk must not mean dead records. The minimum to ask for:

  • Automatic backups without anyone remembering to take them — hourly is a good standard.
  • Several backups kept, so a problem noticed tomorrow can still be rolled back.
  • Automatic recovery: if the database is damaged, the software restores the last good backup on its own.
  • A way to copy backups off the machine.

Which should you choose?

Go cloud if you run many outlets and need a live consolidated view, if aggregator orders dominate and you need deep Swiggy/Zomato integration today, or if your connectivity is excellent and you value phone access above all.

Go offline-first if billing must never stop, if you have one to a few outlets, if you want a cost that does not grow every month, or if you want your data under your own roof.

Why one KOT printer stops working

In a small kitchen, one KOT printer next to the pass is fine: the cook reads the whole ticket and makes everything. The moment you split the kitchen into stations — a tandoor, a Chinese wok station, a cold section, a bar — a single ticket becomes a coordination problem. Someone has to read it out, copy it, or tear it. Items get missed, especially the small ones: one butter naan, a side of raita, a fresh lime soda.

Station routing removes the human relay. The POS knows which station makes which item and prints only those items at that station.

Step 1: list your physical stations

Walk the kitchen and write down every place where food or drink is actually prepared by a different person. Typical Indian restaurant setups:

  • Tandoor — breads, kebabs, tikkas
  • Main kitchen / curry — gravies, dal, rice
  • Chinese / wok — noodles, fried rice, manchurian
  • Cold / desserts — salads, raita, ice cream
  • Bar / beverages — mocktails, lassi, tea and coffee

Keep it to the stations that genuinely exist. Five stations on paper and three in reality means tickets printing at empty counters.

Step 2: give each station an output

Each station needs somewhere for its tickets to appear: its own thermal printer, or a kitchen display screen filtered to that station. For printers, network (Ethernet or Wi-Fi) models are easiest because they can sit anywhere; USB printers have to be near a computer. Name each printer after its station in the operating system — "KOT-TANDOOR", not "POS-80 (2)" — so nobody maps the wrong one.

Step 3: map menu categories to stations

Routing works at the category level: you tell the POS that the "Breads" and "Tandoor Starters" categories belong to the tandoor station, "Chinese Mains" to the wok station, and so on. This is why your menu categories should follow the kitchen, not just the printed menu. If "Starters" contains both paneer tikka (tandoor) and chilli paneer (wok), split it into "Tandoor Starters" and "Chinese Starters" before routing.

Rule of thumb

Every category should be made in exactly one station. If a category spans two stations, split the category.

Step 4: set a default printer

New dishes get added in a hurry, often into a new category nobody mapped. A default KOT printer catches anything without a station, so nothing silently disappears. Check it whenever you add categories.

Step 5: decide what goes on the ticket

A KOT is for the cook, not the guest. It needs:

  • KOT number and time
  • Table and section, or "Takeaway" / "Delivery"
  • Items and quantities, large and readable
  • Modifiers and notes directly under the item ("No onion", "Extra spicy")
  • Server name and covers, if useful

It does not need prices. A cluttered KOT gets misread at speed.

Step 6: test with a real order

Punch one order that touches every station: a naan, a dal, a hakka noodles, a raita and a lime soda. Each station should receive exactly its item, once. Then punch an order with an unmapped item and confirm it reaches the default printer. Then add a second round to the same table and check that only the new items print.

Common mistakes

  • Mapping items instead of categories — fine for ten items, unmanageable at two hundred.
  • Printers on Wi-Fi with a weak signal in a steel-and-tile kitchen. Prefer Ethernet for kitchen printers where you can.
  • Forgetting the bar — drinks printed in the kitchen get made late or not at all.
  • KOT and bill on the same printer at the counter. Give bills their own printer so a long KOT queue never holds up a guest's bill.

Paper or screens?

Once routing is set up, the same mapping can drive kitchen display screens instead of printers. Screens show how long each order has been waiting and let the cook mark it ready; paper survives grease, power cuts and a dead tablet. KDS versus printed KOT compares the two honestly.

How Pass does it

In Pass, you define kitchen stations, map menu categories to them, and assign each station a printer; categories without a station go to the default KOT printer. KOTs are auto-numbered and printed silently on 80mm thermal printers over USB, the network or the system default, with a separate printer for bills. The included kitchen display can be filtered by station and colours tickets by age, turning amber at 8 minutes and red at 15.

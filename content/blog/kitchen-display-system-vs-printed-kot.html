What each one does

A printed KOT is a kitchen order ticket on thermal paper. The POS sends it to a printer at the station; the cook reads it, makes the food, and spikes or bins the slip. It is cheap, fast and familiar, and it tells you nothing after it prints.

A kitchen display system puts the same tickets on a screen. Orders appear as cards. The cook taps to mark an item preparing, then ready. The screen shows how long each order has been waiting, and it can be filtered so the tandoor sees only tandoor items.

Where a KDS genuinely wins

You can see waiting time

The single most useful thing a KDS does is age the ticket. A card that turns amber at eight minutes and red at fifteen makes a forgotten order impossible to miss. Paper cannot do that; a ticket under another ticket is simply invisible.

Nothing gets lost

Slips fall, get wet, or get picked up with the wrong plate. A screen does not lose an order.

The pass knows what is ready

When a station marks its items ready, the person running the pass sees which orders are complete and which are waiting on one dish. That coordination is where a KDS pays for itself in a busy multi-station kitchen.

No paper cost

A busy restaurant can go through several rolls of KOT paper a week. Small, but it adds up.

Where paper still wins

Heat, grease and steam

A tablet next to a tandoor or a wok burner has a short life. Screens need to be mounted away from heat and splashes, which is not always possible in a compact Indian kitchen.

Power cuts

A screen goes dark when the power does. A printed ticket stays readable. If your inverter does not cover the kitchen, paper is the safer default.

Space and hands

A cook with dough on their hands does not want to tap a screen. A slip on a rail asks nothing of them.

Cost

A thermal printer costs a few thousand rupees. A screen needs a tablet or monitor, a mount and something to drive it — and some POS vendors charge a licence per screen on top.

The hybrid most kitchens end up with

In practice, many restaurants settle on paper at the hot stations and one screen at the pass or expo, where the head cook or manager tracks timing and completeness. The stations keep the robustness of paper; the person coordinating gets the visibility of a screen. Your POS needs to support both outputs from the same station routing for this to work.

What to check before buying a KDS

  • Is the kitchen display included, or licensed per screen?
  • Can each screen be filtered to one station?
  • Does it show ticket age, with colour changes at thresholds you can read across a kitchen?
  • Does it work when the internet is down?
  • Can you run printers and screens together?

Setting up a kitchen screen properly

  • Mount it at eye level, away from direct heat, steam and splashes.
  • Use a large enough screen that the cook can read it from their station — not a phone propped against a wall.
  • Filter it to the station it serves, so the cook only sees their items.
  • Put it, and the device driving it, on backup power.
  • Keep a printer configured as a fallback for when the screen is down.
  • Agree who marks orders ready — the station, or the pass.

What the data tells you

A side benefit of screens: they know when an order arrived and when it was marked ready. Over a few weeks that shows which dishes and which stations are slow, and at what time of night the kitchen falls behind. That is information paper never gives you, and it can justify an extra cook on Friday nights — or a simpler menu.

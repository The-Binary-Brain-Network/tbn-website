Composition in one paragraph

The composition scheme is a simplified GST option for small businesses. A restaurant that opts in pays a flat 5% of its turnover as tax — 2.5% CGST and 2.5% SGST — files quarterly instead of monthly, and in exchange gives up two things: it cannot claim input tax credit, and it cannot charge GST to its customers. That second point is the one most billing software gets wrong.

Who can opt in

Restaurant services can use the composition scheme where aggregate turnover in the previous financial year was up to ₹1.5 crore (the limit is lower in a few special-category states). Some businesses cannot opt in at all — for example, those making inter-state supplies of goods or supplying through e-commerce operators that must collect tax at source. Turnover limits and conditions change, so treat these as the starting point for a conversation with your accountant, not the end of it.

Why the bill is different

A regular-scheme restaurant issues a tax invoice showing the taxable value, the CGST and SGST (or IGST) amounts, and the total. A composition restaurant issues a Bill of Supply. Because it is not allowed to collect tax, the Bill of Supply:

  • Is titled Bill of Supply, not tax invoice.
  • Shows no CGST, SGST or IGST lines and no tax breakup.
  • Carries the declaration: "composition taxable person, not eligible to collect tax on supplies".

The customer pays the menu price. The 5% the restaurant owes comes out of that, at the restaurant's expense.

What a correct Bill of Supply looks like

SHREE SAI CAFE
MG Road, Pune 411001

BILL OF SUPPLY
Bill No. SSC-041219-10-2026
Masala Dosa x2220.00
Filter Coffee x280.00
Vada x160.00
TOTAL₹360.00
Composition taxable person, not eligible to collect tax on supplies

Compare that with a regular-scheme invoice for the same order, which would show ₹342.86 taxable value plus ₹8.57 CGST and ₹8.57 SGST — or ₹360 plus ₹9 and ₹9 if the menu prices are tax-exclusive. On a composition bill, none of that appears.

The mistakes we see most

Printing tax lines on a composition bill

The most common error, usually because the POS was set up once with GST at 5% and nobody changed it when the restaurant moved to composition. The customer is being charged a tax the restaurant is not permitted to collect.

Charging menu price plus 5%

Some composition restaurants add 5% "GST" at the bottom of the bill to recover the tax they owe. That is collecting tax by another name, which the scheme forbids.

Missing the declaration

The words are required. A Bill of Supply without them is incomplete.

Mixing schemes across outlets under one PAN

Composition is opted for at the level of the registration, and all registrations under the same PAN generally have to follow the same choice. If you run several outlets, check this before setting up billing.

Composition or regular: a rough guide

Composition tends to suit restaurants with modest turnover, mostly walk-in individual customers (who cannot use input tax credit anyway), and a preference for less paperwork. Regular tends to suit restaurants with significant business customers who want tax invoices, those near the turnover limit, or those planning to grow quickly. The decision is yours and your accountant's; the software's job is to bill correctly under whichever you choose.

Switching your POS to composition

When you change scheme, change the software on the same day:

  1. Switch the tax mode to composition so tax lines disappear.
  2. Check the bill title reads Bill of Supply and the declaration prints.
  3. Check that the GST report separates composition turnover, because that is what your quarterly payment is calculated on.
  4. Print a test bill and keep it with your records.

A simple monthly routine for composition restaurants

  1. Daily: bill every sale through the POS as a Bill of Supply. No handwritten bills.
  2. Monthly: export the sales and GST reports and hand them to your accountant, with purchase bills.
  3. Quarterly: your accountant pays the composition tax on turnover through CMP-08.
  4. Annually: the annual return (GSTR-4) is filed, and you review whether composition still suits your turnover and customer mix.

Watch your turnover through the year. If you are approaching the limit, talk to your accountant before you cross it, not after — switching to the regular scheme mid-year changes how every subsequent bill must be printed.

What the customer sees

Guests sometimes ask why a composition restaurant's bill has no GST on it. A short, accurate answer for staff: "We are registered under the GST composition scheme. We pay the tax on our turnover ourselves, so it isn't added to your bill." Corporate customers who want to claim input tax credit cannot do so on a Bill of Supply, and a few may prefer a regular-scheme restaurant for that reason. It is worth knowing how many of your customers that affects before choosing a scheme.

Composition and online orders

Restaurants supplying through e-commerce operators — the major food delivery platforms — should check current rules carefully with their accountant. The treatment of restaurant services supplied through such platforms, and who collects and pays the tax, has been specifically addressed in GST law, and it affects whether composition remains the right choice for a restaurant with significant delivery volume.

Which TBN products support it

Pass has three tax modes — flat rate, per-item rates, and composition. In composition mode it prints a Bill of Supply with the declaration and no tax lines, and its GST report keeps composition turnover separate. Folio has the same choice for hotels' room-service billing: GST off, Regular (tax invoice with CGST and SGST) or Composition (Bill of Supply). Both print your FSSAI number on every bill.

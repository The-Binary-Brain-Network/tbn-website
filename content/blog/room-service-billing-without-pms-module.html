Why the gap exists

Most hotel management systems were designed around the booking: a room, a rate, a guest, a folio. Food and beverage was assumed to come from a separate restaurant POS that posts charges across. In a hotel with a full restaurant and that POS, the design works. In a 20 to 50-room property where food comes from a small in-house kitchen or a partner restaurant down the road, there is no POS — and so no system for the orders at all.

The symptoms are familiar: orders on a notepad, a running tab in the receptionist's head, a guest disputing a ₹650 dinner at check-out, and a month-end where nobody can say what room service actually earned.

Option 1: paper

A carbon-copy order book and a manual bill. It costs nothing and works until it doesn't: lost slips, unreadable handwriting, no GST invoice, no record of who owes what, and no way to tell whether the kitchen's food cost matches what was billed.

Option 2: a restaurant POS at reception

Better than paper, and right for hotels that also run a restaurant. For room service alone it is an awkward fit. Restaurant POS assumes a table is opened, ordered and billed in an hour. A room needs a tab that stays open for the length of the stay, with orders across several days, a bill at check-out — or later for a corporate guest — and partial payments in between. You also usually need to track what the partner restaurant charged you, which a restaurant POS does not do.

Option 3: replace the PMS

A PMS with a built-in F&B module solves the gap, at the cost of migrating bookings, retraining the front desk and paying for a new system. If your current PMS is otherwise failing you, it is worth considering. If it works well for rooms, replacing it to fix room service is using a hammer on a screw.

Option 4: a room-service tool beside the PMS

A dedicated room-service billing tool runs next to your existing PMS on the same front-desk PC. The PMS keeps doing bookings and check-ins; the room-service tool does what the PMS does not:

  • A tab per room, open from check-in, with a running total.
  • Orders from the in-house kitchen, a partner restaurant, or both in one order.
  • Kitchen and restaurant status tracked from order to delivery.
  • A GST bill per tab at check-out — or a combined bill for a group.
  • Dues for guests and companies who settle later.
  • Cost tracking, so you see the margin on partner-restaurant orders.

The only cost is that two systems run side by side. In practice, the receptionist opens the room-service tab when they check a guest in, and closes it when they check them out. Our room-service setup guide covers the five components of a working setup.

What a working room-service day looks like

  1. Check-in, 2pm: the receptionist checks the guest into the PMS and opens a room-service tab for room 306.
  2. 9.40pm: the guest calls for dinner. The receptionist adds two items from the partner restaurant and one from the in-house kitchen; tickets go to both.
  3. 10.15pm: food delivered; the order is marked delivered. The tab shows the running total.
  4. Next morning: the owner's daily report shows yesterday's room-service revenue, cost and margin.
  5. Check-out: the receptionist closes the tab and prints a GST bill. The guest pays, or the amount moves to dues if their company is paying later.

No notepad, no reconstruction from memory, no dispute.

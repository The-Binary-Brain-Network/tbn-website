The default should be to buy

A good product has been through hundreds of other businesses' edge cases. It is maintained, updated for tax changes, and supported by people who have seen your problem before. It costs a fraction of building, and you can try it this week. If a product covers most of what you need, buy it and adapt the last ten per cent of your process to it.

Five signs you might need custom

1. Your core process is genuinely unusual

Not "we do it slightly differently", but a process no product models: a central kitchen supplying franchisees with its own pricing rules, a hospitality group with an unusual revenue-share arrangement, a service business whose workflow simply isn't hotel or restaurant shaped.

2. The workarounds cost more than building would

Add up the staff hours spent on spreadsheets, double entry and reconciliation every month. If that number over three years exceeds a realistic build cost, the case for custom is real.

3. Systems that need to talk don't

Your POS, your accounting and your inventory each work, but nothing connects them, and the products offer no integration.

4. You need to own it

Some businesses need full control of their software — for regulatory, strategic or valuation reasons.

5. No product works in your conditions

Offline requirements, unusual hardware or language needs that the market does not serve.

What custom software really costs

Building is not just the first version. Budget for:

  • Discovery and specification — understanding the process properly.
  • Build and testing.
  • Deployment and training.
  • Maintenance — bug fixes, tax and rule changes, operating-system updates, every year after.

The last item is the one most often forgotten. Software that nobody maintains decays.

How to buy custom software safely

Insist on a paid discovery phase

A short, paid phase where the developer studies your operation and writes a specification: what the software will do, screen by screen, what it will not do, and how success will be measured. You own the document, so you can take it to another developer if you wish.

Fixed price against fixed scope

With a written specification, a competent developer can quote a fixed price. Hourly billing on an unclear scope transfers all the estimation risk to you, and is how small projects become large invoices.

Own the code

The contract should hand over the source code, database and documentation at the end. Without them, you are locked to one developer forever.

Ship in stages

A first useful version in weeks, not a complete system in a year. Real use finds problems no specification does.

A decision checklist

  • Have you trialled the two best products for your problem?
  • Can you describe, in writing, what they cannot do that you need?
  • Have you costed the workarounds over three years?
  • Do you have a budget for maintenance after launch?
  • Will the contract give you the source code?

If you can tick all five and the products still fall short, custom software is likely justified.

The middle path: product plus small custom pieces

The choice is rarely all-or-nothing. Many businesses get the best result by buying a product for the 90% it covers well and commissioning something small for the 10% it does not:

  • A product for billing, plus a custom report that combines its export with your accounting data.
  • A product for front desk, plus a custom tool for an unusual approval process.
  • A product, plus a small integration that moves data between it and another system you rely on.

Small, well-bounded pieces are cheaper, faster and less risky than a full custom system, and the product vendor keeps maintaining the core.

Red flags when hiring a developer

  • A fixed quote before anyone has studied your process in detail.
  • No written specification, or one written in vague phrases ("user-friendly dashboard").
  • Reluctance to hand over source code.
  • No mention of maintenance after launch.
  • A portfolio entirely of websites when you need business software.
  • No working software they built that you can actually see running.

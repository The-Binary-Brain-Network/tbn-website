Recipe costing and food cost %: the number that decides your margin.

Most restaurants know their rent and their salary bill to the rupee, and their food cost to the nearest guess. Recipe costing turns the guess into a number — per dish and for the month — and recipe-linked inventory keeps it honest.

Short answer

Food cost percentage is the cost of the ingredients in a dish divided by its selling price, times 100. To calculate it, list every ingredient in one portion with its quantity and purchase cost, add them up, and divide by the menu price (before GST). Many Indian restaurants aim for roughly 28 to 35%, but the right target depends on your format.

Step 1: cost one portion

Take one dish and write down every ingredient in a single portion, with the quantity and what that quantity costs you at today's purchase price. Include the things that feel too small to count — oil, butter, garnish, the paper bowl for takeaway.

Example: Paneer Butter Masala, one portion

IngredientQuantityPurchase priceCost
Paneer150 g₹380 / kg₹57.00
Tomato-cashew gravy180 ml₹95 / litre (batch cost)₹17.10
Butter15 g₹520 / kg₹7.80
Cream20 ml₹240 / litre₹4.80
Spices, kasuri methi, garnish—estimate₹3.00
Total₹89.70

Notice the gravy: cost a batch recipe once (all the tomatoes, cashews, onions and spices for 10 litres), divide by the yield, and use that per-litre cost in every dish that uses it.

Step 2: calculate the percentage

If the dish sells for ₹290 before GST:

Food cost % = 89.70 ÷ 290 × 100 = 30.9%

Always use the price before GST. The 5% you collect is not your revenue.

Step 3: price from a target, not from the competitor

If you want this dish at 30% food cost, the price is cost ÷ target: ₹89.70 ÷ 0.30 = ₹299. Round to a menu-friendly ₹295 or ₹299. Pricing this way protects margin when ingredient prices move: when paneer goes to ₹420/kg, the cost becomes ₹95.70 and you can see immediately that the dish has slipped to 33%.

Step 4: the whole-restaurant number

Per-dish costing tells you what should happen. The period number tells you what did:

Actual food cost % = (opening stock + purchases − closing stock) ÷ food sales × 100

If your recipes say 30% and the month comes in at 37%, the seven-point gap is real money leaving somewhere: wastage, over-portioning, staff meals, theft, or recipes that are not followed.

Where the gap usually comes from

  • Portion drift — 150 g of paneer becomes 180 g on a busy night.
  • Wastage not recorded — spoiled cream, burnt naan, yesterday's unsold desserts.
  • Staff meals taken from stock without being logged.
  • Price changes at the supplier that never reached your costing sheet.
  • Complimentary items that are not recorded anywhere.

Recipe-linked inventory: costing that maintains itself

A spreadsheet costing goes stale the week you write it. Recipe-linked inventory does the bookkeeping in the background: each menu item is linked to its ingredients and quantities, and every sale deducts those quantities from stock. You then:

  1. Record purchases as they arrive (ideally by receiving a purchase order into stock).
  2. Record wastage as its own movement, with a note.
  3. Count physical stock weekly for the high-value items — paneer, meat, dairy, oil — and compare with the system.

When physical and system stock disagree consistently for one ingredient, you have found where the money is going.

Targets by format (rough guides)

FormatTypical food cost range
Café, beverage-heavyLower — beverages carry high margins
Casual dining, vegetarianOften around 28–32%
Casual dining, non-vegetarianOften around 30–35%
Seafood, premium meatCan run higher; price accordingly

These are directions, not rules. Your own month-on-month trend is the number to watch.

A monthly food-cost routine

  1. Last night of the month: count closing stock of every high-value ingredient.
  2. First morning: pull the month's purchases and food sales from your system.
  3. Calculate actual food cost % with the formula above.
  4. Compare it with your recipe-based target. Note the gap.
  5. Investigate the two ingredients with the biggest difference between system stock and counted stock.
  6. Update recipe costs for any ingredient whose price changed by more than a few per cent.

Ninety minutes a month. Restaurants that do it consistently usually find a few points of margin within the first quarter.

Common questions

How do you calculate food cost percentage?
Food cost % = (cost of ingredients in one portion ÷ selling price excluding GST) × 100. For the whole restaurant over a period: (opening stock + purchases − closing stock) ÷ food sales × 100.
What is a good food cost percentage for a restaurant in India?
Many full-service restaurants work in the range of about 28 to 35%. Cafés with high beverage sales can run lower; premium seafood or meat-heavy menus often run higher. Track your own trend more closely than any benchmark.
What is recipe-linked inventory?
Recipe-linked inventory connects each menu item to its ingredients and quantities. When the item is sold, the software deducts those ingredients from stock automatically, so stock levels stay current without manual counting after every service.
